Secret Systems  ·  A History of Suppressed Thought

The Secret Systems
of Suppressed Belief

Before there was a First Amendment, there were burned books, buried jars, and imperial law. A sourced history of how early religious pluralism was hunted down — and why that history sits underneath the freedoms Americans now take for granted.

A Reader's History  ·  Setvin Noether

In 1945, an Egyptian farmer digging for fertilizer near the town of Nag Hammadi struck a sealed clay jar. Inside were thirteen leather-bound books, hidden for roughly sixteen centuries. Someone had buried them rather than hand them over. The question worth sitting with is not whether that person was right about theology — it's why hiding a book was ever the safer option.

This is a history of that choice, told through documented law, documented councils, and documented trials — not through the claim that a single hidden hand runs everything. The record is stranger and more specific than that, and it doesn't need embellishing.

I. Before Orthodoxy Had a Name

A Genuinely Plural Beginning

For much of the second century, "Christianity" was not one thing. Communities across Alexandria, Antioch, Rome, and Edessa read different texts, told different stories about who Jesus was, and organized themselves without a shared central authority. The historian Walter Bauer argued in 1934 that what later became "orthodoxy" was not necessarily the earliest or most widespread form of the faith — in several regions, the traditions later branded heretical were the dominant local Christianity before being outcompeted, politically and institutionally, by the Roman-aligned church.

Among these traditions were groups modern scholars group loosely under the term Gnostic — a label of convenience, not a term these groups used for themselves. Texts like the Gospel of Thomas, a collection of sayings attributed to Jesus with no birth narrative, no crucifixion, and no resurrection scene, circulated alongside what would become the canonical gospels. Whether any individual saying in it traces to the historical Jesus is genuinely disputed among scholars — some, following the "Q source" hypothesis, see it preserving very early material; others, like Mark Goodacre, argue it depends on and postdates the canonical gospels. Both positions are held by serious historians today.

"What we call heresies were sometimes, in certain regions, simply the earlier and majority form of the faith."Paraphrased from Walter Bauer, Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity, 1934

Gnostic-leaning groups weren't the only shape dissent took, either, which is worth noting because it shows the pattern wasn't only about cosmology. Montanism, emerging in Phrygia around 157 CE under a prophet named Montanus and two women prophets, Priscilla and Maximilla, claimed ongoing direct revelation through ecstatic prophecy — no dualist cosmology at all, just the much simpler and, to a consolidating church hierarchy, equally threatening claim that God was still speaking outside the control of bishops. It was formally condemned by a synod of Asia Minor bishops around 177 CE. The 2nd-century Christian writer Tertullian, notably, converted to Montanism late in life — a reminder that the line between "orthodox" and "heretic" ran through individual careers, not just between fixed camps.

II. When Theology Became Statute

The Machinery of Imperial Law

The turning point was not a theological debate. It was a legal one. After the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE brought Christianity into alignment with the Roman state, and especially after Emperor Theodosius I made Nicene Christianity the empire's official religion in 380 CE, doctrinal disagreement stopped being a matter of persuasion and became a matter of law.

The legal machinery didn't start with Christian emperors, either — it started with a pagan one, which complicates any story about this being purely a Christian innovation.

302 CE

Diocletian's Edict Against the Manichaeans

Predating Constantine's conversion by decades, Emperor Diocletian — a persecutor of Christians himself — issued the empire's first known legal edict targeting a specific religious minority for its foreign origin and teachings, ordering Manichaean leaders and their books burned. The precedent (foreign teaching = legal threat) was pagan Roman before it was ever Christian. Text preserved in the Collatio Legum Mosaicarum et Romanarum, 15.3.

367 CE

Athanasius's Festal Letter

The Bishop of Alexandria issued a letter defining an official canon of scripture and calling for the destruction of texts outside it. Scholars widely connect the timing to the Nag Hammadi burial — someone, somewhere in Upper Egypt, chose to hide rather than surrender their books around this period.

380 CE

Edict of Thessalonica

Theodosius I made Nicene Christianity the mandatory state religion, formally defining all other Christian teaching as legally deviant.

c. 373–383 CE

Augustine's Manichaean Decade

Before he was orthodoxy's most influential theologian, Augustine of Hippo spent roughly nine years as a Manichaean — a dualist faith, distinct from but often historically conflated with Gnosticism, that also held the material world to be a battleground between light and dark forces. His Confessions (Book 3–5, c. 397 CE) describes his eventual disillusionment and conversion to Nicene Christianity, after which he became the tradition's sharpest anti-Manichaean and anti-Donatist polemicist. It's a genuinely useful case for the "who decides orthodoxy" question this whole history keeps raising: the man who helped define what heresy meant for the next thousand years spent a decade of his own life inside a movement later law would have criminalized.

311–411 CE

The Donatist Controversy

A North African dispute, not about cosmology at all but about whether clergy who had handed over scriptures during earlier Roman persecution could validly administer sacraments afterward. What makes it significant here: Augustine, arguing against the Donatists, became the first major Christian theologian to write a systematic justification for using state coercion against fellow Christians, citing Luke 14:23's "compel them to come in" — a text and an argument later cited directly by medieval inquisitors justifying force against the Cathars. The theological machinery for religious coercion, in other words, was built by the same figure canonized as a Doctor of the Church.

385 CE

The Execution of Priscillian

A Spanish bishop whose ascetic teaching drew accusations of magic and Manichaeism, Priscillian was tried by a secular Roman court and beheaded at Trier — the first documented case of Christian authorities executing another Christian for heresy. Even contemporaries were uneasy about it: both Ambrose of Milan and Pope Siricius publicly objected to the involvement of secular courts in a doctrinal matter, which tells us the norm was not yet fully settled even among the orthodox.

438 CE

Codex Theodosianus, Book 16

The compiled imperial law code included a section, De Haereticis, that criminalized possession of specific banned texts, barred those convicted of heresy from inheritance, public office, and military service, and authorized property seizure from anyone sheltering a dissenter.

529–534 CE

Justinian's Codex and the Closure of the Athenian Academy

Justinian's legal compilation went further than Theodosius's, stripping unbaptized persons and heretics of the right to testify in court against orthodox citizens or hold public office — codified civil death. The same emperor's 529 CE decree shut down the last major institutional home of classical philosophical teaching in the Greek world, ending centuries of continuous instruction.

553 CE

Origen's Posthumous Condemnation

The Second Council of Constantinople anathematized the theologian Origen of Alexandria — dead for nearly three centuries by that point — for teaching positions including the pre-existence of souls and a form of universal salvation. Origen had been, in his lifetime, one of the church's most prolific and respected biblical scholars; the delay between his death and his condemnation shows orthodoxy wasn't a fixed line but a boundary still being actively redrawn centuries into Christian history, sometimes retroactively.

Between Augustine's Manichaean years and his later justification of coercion, and Origen's centuries-delayed condemnation, a pattern worth sitting with emerges: the line separating orthodoxy from heresy wasn't fixed at any single moment, and the people who eventually enforced it had sometimes stood on the other side of it themselves.

None of this required a conspiracy. It required a state church with the power to write law, and it used that power the way most institutions use durable power: to entrench itself and remove competitors. Historian Elaine Pagels made the more specific case in The Gnostic Gospels (1979) that the fight was as much about organizational structure as doctrine — Gnostic communities in some cases operated with rotating leadership and no fixed priesthood, which was simply incompatible with a church trying to consolidate around bishops.

A counter-argument worth taking seriously: historian Peter Brown has cautioned against reading the Theodosian Code as evidence of efficient, pervasive enforcement. Ancient states had limited administrative reach — laws could sit on the books largely unenforced outside a few high-profile cases, and Brown's Power and Persuasion in Late Antiquity (1992) argues local bishops often had more practical influence over a community's beliefs than distant imperial edicts did. The law existed. How consistently it was actually applied on the ground, versus invoked selectively against inconvenient rivals, is a separate and less settled question.

III. The Crusade at Home

Cathars, Councils, and the Birth of the Inquisition

Dualist and ascetic Christian movements resurfaced in medieval Europe with no direct institutional link to the ancient Gnostics — the theological resemblance (a flawed material world, a hidden spiritual truth) appears to be independent reinvention, not inheritance. The most famous were the Cathars of southern France.

1209–1229

The Albigensian Crusade

Pope Innocent III called a military crusade against fellow Christians in Languedoc. Historian Malcolm Lambert's The Cathars (1998) documents the scale: tens of thousands killed, including the notorious sack of Béziers in 1209.

1215 CE

Fourth Lateran Council

Convened by Innocent III, the council made annual private confession to a parish priest mandatory for all adult Christians (Canon 21) — a durable surveillance mechanism at the level of individual conscience, not just public behavior, that outlasted the Inquisition itself by centuries.

1231–1252

The Permanent Papal Inquisition

Pope Gregory IX's bull Ille Humani Generis established standing tribunals staffed by trained Dominican inquisitors. Pope Innocent IV's 1252 bull Ad Extirpanda formally authorized torture in questioning.

1179–1215 CE

The Waldensians

Founded by the Lyon merchant Peter Waldo, this movement is frequently confused with the Cathars in popular accounts, but historian Euan Cameron's Waldenses (2000) is emphatic that they were doctrinally orthodox on cosmology — no Demiurge, no dualism. Their actual offense was vernacular scripture translation and unlicensed lay preaching, including by women. They were excommunicated at the Third Lateran Council (1179) for insubordination, not heresy of belief, which is a meaningfully different charge that later polemic blurred.

1478–1834 CE

The Spanish Inquisition

Distinct from the medieval papal Inquisition, this was a royal institution under Ferdinand and Isabella, initially targeting converted Jews (conversos) and Muslims (moriscos) suspected of secretly practicing their former faith. Historian Henry Kamen's revisionist The Spanish Inquisition: A Historical Revision (1997) argues its reputation for scale was later inflated by Protestant and Enlightenment polemicists — a genuinely contested historiographical point that cuts against the instinct to assume every account of persecution is understated rather than sometimes overstated.

1600 CE

Giordano Bruno

The philosopher, tried by the Roman Inquisition for a cluster of heterodox positions including a plural, infinite cosmos, was burned in Rome's Campo de' Fiori after seven years of imprisonment.

1559 CE

Index Librorum Prohibitorum

Pope Paul IV formalized a standing, updated list of forbidden books, enforced by the Roman Church into the 20th century — it was not formally abolished until 1966.

The through-line across these events is not mysticism. It's what historian R.I. Moore called, in The Formation of a Persecuting Society (1987), the invention of durable bureaucratic tools for tracking and punishing dissent — record-keeping, denunciation networks, standing courts — built originally to find heretics and later repurposed by secular states for other kinds of political policing entirely. Moore's thesis is itself an argument, not a neutral fact: he's making a specific claim that persecution became structurally embedded in Western institutions around the 12th and 13th centuries, a claim other medievalists have contested on the grounds that it may understate persecution in earlier periods to make the medieval shift look sharper than it was.

IV. What the Founders Actually Knew

The First Amendment as a Direct Reply to This History

This is the part of the story that doesn't require speculation, because the men who wrote it left an extensive paper trail. Thomas Jefferson and James Madison were both trained in classical and European history, and both wrote explicitly about the danger of state-enforced religious conformity — not as an abstraction, but with direct reference to the Inquisition, the European wars of religion, and the persecution of dissenting sects.

"Rulers who wished to subvert the public liberty may have found an established clergy convenient auxiliaries. A just government, instituted to secure and perpetuate it, needs them not."James Madison, Memorial and Remonstrance Against Religious Assessments, 1785

Jefferson's Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom (drafted 1777, enacted 1786) was the direct template for the First Amendment's religion clauses. In it, Jefferson wrote that compelling a person to support opinions they disbelieve is "sinful and tyrannical," and that civil rights should have no dependence on religious opinion any more than on opinions in physics or geometry. He considered this achievement significant enough to have it listed, alongside authoring the Declaration of Independence, on his own gravestone — he left the presidency off the list.

Jefferson also famously took a razor to the New Testament, physically cutting out the miracle accounts and the resurrection to leave only Jesus's ethical teaching — a project he called, in private letters, an attempt to separate "diamonds" from a "dunghill" of later additions by what he saw as self-interested clergy. It's worth being precise here: Jefferson was a theist who believed in a rational, non-intervening creator (a position historians call Deism), not a Gnostic in the ancient sense — he had no access to Nag Hammadi, discovered more than a century after his death, and he did not believe the material world was a flawed trap. What he shared with the older suppressed traditions was narrower and more specific: a conviction that institutional religious authority should never again have the power of the state behind it.

Jefferson and Madison were not working from a blank slate philosophically, either. Both drew directly on John Locke's A Letter Concerning Toleration (1689), written in the wake of England's own religious civil wars, which argued that civil government has no legitimate authority over "the salvation of souls" because belief cannot be compelled by force in any case — a coerced confession is worthless as genuine faith. And the colonies already had a working domestic precedent: Roger Williams's Rhode Island colony, founded in 1636 explicitly on the principle of a "wall of separation" between civil and religious authority (Williams's own phrase, which Jefferson borrowed verbatim in his 1802 letter to the Danbury Baptists), after Williams himself had been banished from Massachusetts Bay for religious dissent. The Founders weren't reasoning about persecution in the abstract — they had a domestic case study a century and a half old.

Williams's actual argument survives in detail because he fought it out in print. His The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution (1644) was written as a direct response to Massachusetts minister John Cotton, who had defended the colony's authority to banish and punish religious dissenters on the grounds that a godly commonwealth had a duty to protect itself from corruption — essentially a Protestant American restatement of the same "compel them to come in" logic Augustine had used against the Donatists thirteen centuries earlier. Cotton wrote back (The Bloudy Tenent, Washed and Made White, 1647); Williams answered again. The exchange is one of the few places in this entire history where both the persecutor's argument and the dissenter's rebuttal survive in full and in the dissenter's own words, rather than only through a hostile source's summary.

Madison's fingerprints on institutional religious liberty predate the First Amendment by two years. At the 1787 Constitutional Convention, he supported what became Article VI of the Constitution, which states that no religious test shall ever be required as a qualification for any federal office — a narrower but more immediately binding provision than the First Amendment, since it applied the moment the Constitution was ratified in 1788, before the Bill of Rights existed at all.

Worth noting

Many prominent Founders — Washington, Franklin, Revere, Hancock — were Freemasons, and Enlightenment-era Freemasonry's higher degrees did draw on Hermetic and Kabbalistic symbolism inherited via the Renaissance. But there's a real gap between "used shared esoteric vocabulary" and "secretly practiced ancient Gnosticism," and the historical record supports only the former. It's a case where the more modest claim is also the more interesting one: these were Enlightenment rationalists building civic institutions, using old architectural metaphors, not a hidden mystery cult running the republic.

V. Where Historians Actually Disagree

Four Live Arguments, Not Four Settled Facts

It's worth being specific about what's disputed and by whom, because the disagreements are more informative than a false consensus would be.

Does the Gospel of Thomas contain the historical Jesus's own words?

Three camps, roughly. Independence advocates (Stephen Patterson, and earlier work associated with the Jesus Seminar) argue Thomas draws on a sayings tradition that circulated separately from, and possibly earlier than, the canonical gospels — structurally similar to the hypothesized "Q source" behind Matthew and Luke. Dependence advocates (Mark Goodacre, Christopher Tuckett) argue the opposite: close textual analysis shows Thomas reworking material already shaped by Matthew and Luke's editorial choices, meaning Thomas is downstream, not upstream. April DeConick proposed a middle position — a "rolling corpus" theory, where an early core of sayings was genuinely old, but the text kept absorbing new material for decades after, meaning any individual saying has to be evaluated on its own rather than the document being treated as uniformly early or uniformly late. No one has resolved this. It's an open textual argument conducted in peer-reviewed journals, not a settled fact in either direction.

How much continuity is there between ancient dualism and the Cathars?

Popular treatments often draw a straight line: Gnostics → Paulicians → Bogomils → Cathars, one underground river. Specialist historians are considerably more cautious. Bernard Hamilton and other Byzantinists have traced plausible missionary contact between Bogomil communities and Western Europe in the 11th and 12th centuries, which would support some transmission. But R.I. Moore, in The War on Heresy (2012), argues the evidence for a continuous underground dualist tradition reaching back to antiquity is thin, and that Catharism may be better explained as a home-grown reaction to specific 12th-century conditions in Languedoc — clerical corruption, rapid urbanization — that produced dualist-sounding theology independently, with the label "Manichean" applied by hostile chroniclers who reached for the most available ancient insult rather than accurately describing a real lineage. Moore's argument was itself controversial when published and has not settled the question.

Was Walter Bauer right that "orthodoxy" wasn't first?

Bauer's 1934 thesis was radical for its time and remains influential, but it's not uncontested. H.E.W. Turner and later Thomas Robinson pushed back, arguing Bauer overstated his case in specific regions (particularly Edessa) where the surviving evidence is thinner than Bauer treated it. The current scholarly middle ground, associated with work compiled in Bart Ehrman's Lost Christianities (2003), accepts that early Christianity was more doctrinally plural than the traditional narrative allowed, while being more cautious than Bauer was about claiming heterodoxy was regionally dominant everywhere he argued it was.

Was Jefferson's razor an act of recovery or an act of erasure?

The conventional framing treats The Jefferson Bible as Jefferson stripping away later corruption to recover an authentic ethical core — his own words describe it that way. But this is Jefferson's own claim about his own project, not a neutral historical description, and it's worth reading with the same skepticism applied to any other primary source with a stake in its own narrative. A different, less flattering reading is available: Jefferson wasn't recovering anything, he was constructing a Jesus that matched his own Enlightenment ethics and discarding what didn't fit — which is a defensible thing to do as a matter of personal conscience, but a different claim than "finding what was really there."

Did Marcion, not the proto-orthodox church, invent the idea of a fixed New Testament canon?

Marcion of Sinope (c. 85–160 CE) proposed the first known defined Christian canon — a purged Luke and ten Pauline letters — while rejecting the Hebrew Bible entirely on the grounds that its creator-god was a lesser, harsher deity than the God revealed by Jesus, a position with real structural overlap with later Gnostic Demiurge theology, though Marcion is usually classified separately from the Gnostics proper. Historian Adolf von Harnack argued in the early 20th century that Marcion's canon forced the proto-orthodox church to respond by finalizing its own canon defensively — meaning the concept of "the Bible" as a bounded list may owe its existence to a theologian the mainstream church excommunicated. This remains debated: more recent scholars like David Trobisch propose earlier or more organic canon formation, and Judith Lieu's Marcion and the Making of a Heretic (2015) is more cautious than Harnack about crediting Marcion as the sole catalyst.

How reliable is Irenaeus as a source for what the groups he condemned actually believed?

Nearly everything known about many Gnostic teachers before 1945 came through Irenaeus's Adversus Haereses — a text written explicitly to refute them, not describe them fairly. When the Nag Hammadi texts surfaced, scholars got a rare chance to check a hostile secondary source against primary material, and the results were mixed: Irenaeus's account of Valentinian cosmology turned out to be broadly accurate in outline, per Bentley Layton's textual comparisons, but selectively simplified and rhetorically sharpened in ways that made the system sound more absurd than the primary texts do on their own terms. The lesson generalizes uncomfortably: a hostile source can be factually right about the broad shape of a belief system and still be a distorting lens for understanding it.

VI. 1945 to Now

Recovery, Not Revelation

The Nag Hammadi codices sat in scholarly and political limbo for decades after their discovery — antiquities disputes, Cold War-era academic turf wars, and slow translation work delayed full public access until James M. Robinson's complete English edition in 1977. That delay is well documented and mundane: institutional friction, not suppression by decree.

What followed was a real, open scholarly argument that continues today. Elaine Pagels made the case for the political stakes of the original suppression. Hans Jonas connected ancient Gnostic alienation to 20th-century existentialism. Bentley Layton and Marvin Meyer produced the standard modern translations. None of them claim a single continuous hidden lineage running from ancient Alexandria to the present — the actual scholarly consensus is closer to independent recurrence: societies under enough administrative or imperial strain seem to keep re-arriving at similar anti-institutional, "the visible authority is not the real authority" ideas, without needing a secret chain of transmission to explain why.

That's a more interesting claim than a conspiracy, not a less interesting one. It suggests something about how human institutions behave under centralized pressure — repeatedly, in unconnected times and places — rather than about one hidden group pulling one long thread.

VII. A Few Lenses Worth Trying On

Modern Thinkers Who Picked Up the Same Shape

None of what follows is offered as continuity — there's no evidence these later thinkers were consciously extending an ancient lineage, and the honest scholarly position, argued above, is independent recurrence rather than transmission. But the shape keeps showing up in serious, citable work, and it's worth trying each lens on rather than deciding in advance whether it fits.

Carl Jung: the myth as a map of the mind, not the cosmos

Jung read Gnostic cosmology not as a claim about the physical universe but as an externalized diagram of the individuation process — the Archons as personifications of psychological complexes standing between a person and self-knowledge, the Pleroma as a symbol for psychic wholeness rather than a place. His Seven Sermons to the Dead (1916) and the posthumously published Red Book read like Jung generating his own Gnostic-style cosmology from active imagination. Whether this tells you something true about the human mind, or simply tells you that one influential 20th-century psychologist found the vocabulary useful, is a judgment call worth making for yourself rather than accepting on authority — his own or anyone else's.

Eric Voegelin: when "gnosis" becomes a political diagnosis

Voegelin's Science, Politics, and Gnosticism (1968) argued that modern mass ideological movements — he had fascism and Marxism specifically in mind — reenact a Gnostic structure: a conviction that ordinary reality is fundamentally corrupt, that a small group possesses the special knowledge needed to fix it, and that fixing it justifies overriding ordinary constraints. It's a provocative comparison and a contested one — critics point out that stretching "Gnostic" to cover any totalizing political movement risks making the word mean everything and therefore nothing. Worth reading Voegelin directly rather than secondhand, and worth asking where the analogy holds and where it's being asked to do too much work.

Jacques Ellul: technique as an environment nobody voted for

Ellul's The Technological Society (1954) isn't about religion at all — it's a sociological argument that modern life is organized around la technique, the relentless pursuit of the most efficient method, which becomes self-perpetuating and unaccountable to the individuals living inside it. Ellul was a practicing Christian theologian as well as a sociologist, and he was aware of the structural resemblance between his argument and older anti-cosmic religious critique — a system that nobody designed with malice, that nonetheless behaves as though it has its own interests. Reading Ellul next to Irenaeus's ancient opponents is an exercise in analogy, not evidence of shared origin, but the analogy earns its keep: it gives you a vocabulary for describing institutional behavior that doesn't require assuming anyone is a villain.

Jean Baudrillard: the map that replaces the territory

Baudrillard's Simulacra and Simulation (1981) argues that mediated, mass-produced representations of reality can become more determinative of people's lives than the underlying reality itself — the map doesn't just represent the territory, it replaces it. This is a claim about media and semiotics, not theology, and Baudrillard wasn't working from Gnostic sources. The resemblance to older "the visible world is a screen over the real one" language is a case where a real 20th-century French theorist arrived, independently and through entirely different intellectual roads, at a structurally similar diagnosis of how a managed representation of reality can substitute for direct contact with it.

How to use this section

The point of laying these four out together isn't to claim they're all saying the same thing, or that one hidden truth connects a 2nd-century Egyptian sect to a 20th-century French sociologist. It's to notice that "the visible authority isn't the real authority, and the ordinary person is meant to stay unaware of that" is a genuinely recurring diagnostic shape across very different fields, centuries, and starting assumptions — and to let you decide for yourself whether that recurrence is meaningful, coincidental, or just what pattern-seeking minds do with enough historical distance. All four are real, citable, disagreed-about arguments. None of them are settled, and neither is the question of whether they belong in the same conversation at all.

VIII. Open Rather Than Closed

Questions Worth Sitting With

A few threads this history leaves genuinely open, rather than settled:

Why does the pattern recur? Bogomils in Byzantium, Cathars in Languedoc, and Gnostics in Roman Egypt shared no confirmed direct contact, yet arrived at structurally similar critiques of centralized religious authority. Is that convergent reasoning under similar institutional pressure, or does it say something about a genuine tension built into any sufficiently centralized system of belief?

Does legal protection change the social cost? The First Amendment ended state prosecution for heterodox belief in America. It didn't, and structurally couldn't, end informal social cost — professional risk, reputational risk, the freelance work of ridicule. Is that residual cost a bug in an otherwise-finished project, or an irreducible feature of any society that still has to agree on what counts as normal?

What was actually lost? We don't know what fraction of second-century Christian writing was destroyed versus simply not copied enough to survive ordinary manuscript attrition. Historians are honest that this is unresolved. What would the shape of the historical record need to look like for us to ever know the difference?

Who gets to define the category? Karen King has argued, in What Is Gnosticism? (2003), that "Gnosticism" as a single coherent religion may be partly a modern scholarly invention, built by generalizing across groups that ancient heresiologists like Irenaeus lumped together for polemical convenience rather than because they shared a common origin or self-identity. If that's right, some of what this article treats as "the Gnostic tradition" may really be several unrelated traditions that only look unified because their opponents filed them under the same heading. Does the category still do useful work if that's true, or does it need retiring?

Does calling something "heresy" ever describe anything other than the loser of a political contest? In every case surveyed here, the losing side's texts were destroyed, so we only have the winning side's account of why the losers were wrong. That's a structural, not incidental, bias in the historical record. What would it take to write this history from a genuinely neutral position, and is that even possible when one side controlled what got copied for the next thousand years?
